THE LONG TABLE

The table came before the house did. My grandfather built it in a rented garage the winter they were married, out of two doors and a beam he had been given for hauling away, and it moved with them eleven times before it stopped.

I learned to write at that table, which is to say I learned to wait at it. Bread rose under a towel at one end. Bills waited at the other.

Editor's note (week 6): the chapter opens on the table and ends on the bills. Consider letting the towel come back once, near the end.

Nobody in the family remembers who first called it the long table, only that no one has ever called it anything else.

The ninety days

A shape you can keep to.

Most first books fail in the middle, not at the start. The program is built around the middle.

Week 1

The backbone

You answer a structured interview about the book you mean to write and give us a few pages of anything you have already written, an email, a talk, a letter. From that, the Writer drafts a chapter-by-chapter outline in your own turns of phrase. You cut it, reorder it, and make it yours. This is the first of only two places AI touches your book.

Weeks 2–11

You write

One chapter file at a time, in an editor made for books: chapter status marks, notes to yourself that never print, images placed where they belong, and a weekly target that fits the length you chose. At any point, press Proof Book and see the manuscript as real 6 × 9 spreads with running heads and page numbers, or as a reflowable ebook. Watching the pages fill is the best motivator we know.

Week 12

The developmental read

When the draft is whole, the Writer reads all of it against the backbone you approved and returns an editor's report: where the argument drops a thread, where a chapter promises something the next one forgets, where the evidence is thin, what is repeated, what is missing. It suggests; it does not rewrite. This is the second and last place AI touches your book.

Week 13

Revision and files

You revise. Then the press does its part: a typeset 6 × 9 interior in an openly licensed typeface you choose from a short list, a reflowable EPUB, and a cover built with you. Files are checked the way we check our own titles.

Day 90

A printed book

H.D. Publishing publishes the paperback and ebook under its imprint, with an ISBN, unless you would rather publish it yourself, in which case you leave with the files. Either way, a printed copy arrives at your door.

Where the machine stops

Two touchpoints. Never the prose.

The book has to be yours, and it has to be honestly yours: under the retailers' rules a book whose text you wrote, with AI used to structure and critique it, needs no disclosure. A book the machine drafted does.

  • Drafts the outline from your interview and your samples, in your phrasing, for you to change.
  • Learns your voice from what you give it and from your own chapters as they arrive, and uses that only to speak to you in your register.
  • Reads the finished draft and reports on structure, argument, evidence, repetition, and gaps.
  • Keeps the whole book in view, so the report checks chapter nine against what chapter two promised.
  • Never writes a sentence of your book. There is no "generate chapter" button, and there will not be one.
  • Never rewrites you. Suggestions arrive as notes in the margin, not as replaced text.
  • Never trains on your manuscript for anyone else. Your voice profile serves your book and no other.
  • Never publishes for you. Every file goes out under your name, after your approval, with a person at the press behind it.
What you get

The software, the schedule, and the press.

An editor built for books

One file per chapter, status marks from build to done, private notes that never print, images and plates in place, manual page breaks, and a proof view of the real pages at any moment.

A schedule that holds

A weekly target sized to your book, a backbone to write against, and the two editorial touchpoints at the start and the end. Ninety days is long enough to write a real book and short enough to finish one.

A publisher at the end

A 6 × 9 paperback and a reflowable EPUB, an ISBN, a copyright page that names you, distribution through the usual channels and our own bookshop, and a printed copy in your hands.

Questions

Before you sign up.

Who owns the book?

You do. Copyright stays with you. H.D. Publishing publishes under a plain-language agreement you will read before you pay for anything, and if you would rather publish elsewhere you leave with your files.

What kind of books is this for?

Nonfiction, first. Memoir, family history, a professional's book, a guide to something you know. The first cohort is nonfiction only; fiction follows when we have done it well once.

What does it cost?

A monthly subscription for as long as you are writing, with no charge for the publishing step itself. The figure is set with the first cohort and put in writing to you before you commit. There is no fee for the ISBN and no purchase requirement for your own copies.

Will readers be able to tell AI was involved?

No, because it was not involved in the words. The outline is yours after you edit it, and the editor's report is a report, not text. Under the retailers' definitions the result is your work with AI assistance, which needs no disclosure. We keep to that line deliberately.

How is my manuscript stored?

In your account, on our servers, as plain chapter files you can download at any time. Your manuscript is not used to train anything for anyone else.

What if I need more than ninety days?

Then you take them. The ninety days are a structure, not a deadline; the subscription runs until you say stop.

How do I sign in?

Accounts run through our bookshop's customer login. Once you have a place in a cohort, your invitation carries the link. Existing authors sign in here.

Can I bring a book I have already started?

Yes. The backbone step works from whatever exists, and chapters paste in as files. The schedule shortens accordingly.
