The Mirror Clause

One court. Two orders. Ten years. And a mirror that asks whose reflection it is.

On Wednesday, December 10, 2025, at 9:15 in the morning, in a courtroom in Benton County, Washington, a judge, an attorney, and one witness entered a parenting plan that found one parent an abuser of the children, on two exhibits, with that parent not in the room. No notice had reached that parent. No evidence was tested at a hearing.

Court was in recess at 9:25. Ten minutes. This book is what the ten minutes left out.

The parent who was not in the room moved, without a lawyer, to vacate the plan. On June 4, 2026, the court found its own notice legally deficient, vacated the plan, and re-entered it the same day as a temporary order, every finding intact. A new trial was set.

It is not a memoir. It is a review of one public court file, laid out the way a file ought to be handed and never is: the persons and the documents first, then eleven years of dates in a row, then two parenting plans ten years apart with the names covered.

One plan restricted a parent after a gunshot. The other restricted a parent after a scuffle over a phone. The clause that governs alcohol in both is the same clause, with the names reversed. That clause gives the book its title.

Uncover the names and one parent stands on both sides of the mirror: the same person, protected by the first plan, restricted by the second, come before the same court a decade later. The book does not tell the reader what to make of that. It sets the two files side by side, line for line, and asks what changed between them, and whether it should have.

Written in the manner of the classic detective novel, the book asks the reader to take the one chair no one in a family case is ever offered: the neutral one.

Every fact, quotation, and date is from the record and cited to it by date and docket index. Every interpretation is marked as the author's. Sealed reports are described and not fully reproduced. The statutes that decide a parenting plan are printed in the Legislature's own words.

On October 28, 2026, a court will hear the same petition again. Before it does, the book puts the questions that trial must answer to the reader, one at a time. It closes with a relief requested: fourteen gaps in Washington family-court practice that the file passed through, each set beside the statute that governs it and one sentence on what would close it.

If what lies in these pages can happen to one parent, it can happen to another.
